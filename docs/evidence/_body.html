02 — Transport

QUIC’s climb to default carriage

By early 2026, HTTP/3 had crossed the line from a frontier experiment into the silent default for a large share of public web traffic. Crawls of the top-ten-million origins put protocol availability near a third of all hostnames [[3]], but the volume-weighted picture is far higher: because the busiest properties—CDNs, search, social, video—turned QUIC on first, the share of requests served over HTTP/3 now sits well above the share of sites that offer it [[7]].

The asymmetry is the whole story. A single large CDN flipping a default moves more bytes than a million long-tail blogs ever will, which is why edge-network telemetry and origin-crawl telemetry disagree so sharply on the headline number [[12]]. Both are correct; they are simply counting different things. For an operator deciding whether to invest, the request-weighted figure is the one that predicts user-visible latency wins.

What still throttles the long tail is operational, not protocol-level. UDP throughput on commodity kernels lags TCP without tuning, middlebox handling of QUIC remains uneven on enterprise egress paths, and many managed hosting stacks simply have not exposed an HTTP/3 toggle to their customers yet [[19]]. None of these are cryptographic or standards gaps—they are the unglamorous deployment frictions that always trail a transport change by a few years.
