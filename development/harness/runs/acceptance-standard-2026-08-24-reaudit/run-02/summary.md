# AI coding agents in production: productivity, reliability, security, and evidence vs vendor claims

## Executive Summary

AI coding agents in production show a consistent pattern of strong adoption paired with uneven evidence on outcomes, where measured productivity, reliability and security results diverge sharply from marketing claims [14] [3] [22]. Controlled lab studies have reported large speed gains for AI-assisted developers, while trials on experienced developers working in their own mature repositories and large-scale field studies have found no throughput gain, slower completion, and higher defect rates [2] [3] [6]. Security evaluations at both user-study and large-scale model-testing scale find that AI assistance increases insecure code and that nearly half of AI-generated code samples introduce vulnerabilities, with trust in AI output falling even as usage rises [12] [22] [14].

Reliability assessment is constrained by benchmark design, where the most cited measure of real-world issue resolution, SWE-bench Verified, relies on existing repository tests that can accept plausible but incorrect patches, and where independent re-evaluation has documented meaningful false-positive rates [18] [38]. Vendor-reported scores on this benchmark are systematically higher than independent standardized re-runs, with gaps of 15 to 35 points documented across model families and scaffolds, and with early high-profile claims based on subsampled evaluations that are not directly comparable to full-dataset baselines [42] [45] [28]. The DORA research program finds that AI adoption alone does not improve delivery performance and can reduce throughput and stability without strong underlying engineering practices, framing AI as an amplifier of existing strengths and weaknesses rather than a standalone fix [8] [32].

Taken together, the evidence suggests that productivity benefits are task- and context-dependent, that reliability and security costs are material and often underestimated in vendor narratives, and that economic return depends on managing a verification tax and an initial productivity dip through platform quality, small batches, and robust testing [37] [8] [48]. The decisive uncertainty is not whether agents can generate functional code quickly in isolated tasks, but whether organizations can absorb the review, testing, and maintenance burden required to make that code safe and maintainable at scale [37] [12] [38].

## Productivity: from lab acceleration to field stagnation

The earliest quantitative claim for AI coding assistance comes from GitHub, where developers using GitHub Copilot completed an identical programming task in an average of 1 hour 11 minutes versus 2 hours 41 minutes for developers without Copilot, a 55% faster completion rate [2] [1]. The same body of work reported that between 60% and 75% of users felt more fulfilled and less frustrated, and that 73% reported staying in flow and 87% reported preserving mental effort on repetitive tasks [2]. This framing presents productivity holistically, linking speed to satisfaction and reduced cognitive load, and it was positioned around typical tasks designed to reflect everyday development scenarios [2]. The confidence interval for the speed gain was reported as 21% to 89% with statistical significance at P=.0017, indicating a wide range around the headline 55% figure even within the lab setting [2] [1].

A directly contrasting result comes from METR, where experienced open-source developers working on real issues in their own mature repositories took 19% longer to complete tasks when allowed to use early-2025 AI tools [3] [4]. The same developers estimated on average that AI had made them 20% faster, illustrating a divergence between perceived acceleration and measured completion time [3]. METR itself labels this result as historical and not necessarily reflective of current tools or workflows, emphasizing that it is one study in a specific setting rather than settled consensus [3]. The tension between the GitHub and METR results is not a simple contradiction to be resolved by choosing one number, but a signal that task type, codebase familiarity, and developer experience materially shape outcomes [2] [3].

Field data from Uplevel Data Labs, which examined 800 developers on large engineering teams before and after GitHub Copilot adoption with a test group with access and a control group without, found little to no productivity gain on delivery metrics such as pull request cycle time and throughput [5] [6]. That work also found that the group with Copilot access introduced 41% more bugs in pull requests while issue throughput remained consistent, suggesting a negative impact on code quality without a compensating speed gain [6] [7]. A separate summary of the same research notes that Copilot access did not mitigate burnout risk, with the sustained-always-on metric decreasing by 17% for those with access versus 28% for those without [5] [6]. The implication is that even when individual task speed improves, the downstream costs of fixing defects and reviewing additional code can erase throughput gains at the team level [5] [6].

The DORA program, based on annual surveys of thousands of technology professionals, provides a broader organizational lens that helps reconcile these divergent findings [8] [9]. The 2024 DORA report found that a 25% increase in AI adoption was associated with a 7.5% increase in documentation quality, a 3.4% increase in code quality, and a 3.1% increase in code review speed, yet it also estimated a 1.5% decrease in delivery throughput and a 7.2% reduction in delivery stability as AI adoption increased [8]. The report explicitly states that improving the development process does not automatically improve software delivery without adherence to fundamentals like small batch sizes and robust testing mechanisms [8]. By 2025, DORA reported that AI adoption had surged to 90% with a median of two hours daily spent with AI, and that over 80% of respondents indicated productivity gains and 59% reported positive influence on code quality, while also finding that AI amplifies existing organizational strengths and weaknesses rather than fixing struggling teams [9] [32].

Taken together, the evidence suggests that speed gains are most robust in short, well-specified, repetitive tasks in controlled environments, while complex, repository-specific work with experienced developers shows no gain or a net slowdown once review and debugging are included [2] [3] [5]. The convergence between METR and Uplevel on the gap between perception and measurement, and between DORA 2024 and Uplevel on the lack of delivery-level improvement, contrasts with the convergence between GitHub's lab result and DORA 2025's self-reported productivity gains, highlighting that self-report and satisfaction do not reliably predict throughput [3] [6] [9]. For engineering leaders, the practical implication is that productivity should be assessed at the delivery system level, not the individual suggestion level, and that gains in documentation quality and review speed do not automatically translate into faster, more stable releases [8].

The divergence also reflects differences in what is being measured, as GitHub's lab task isolates coding time on a greenfield-style problem, while METR and Uplevel capture the full cycle of understanding context, integrating changes, and fixing introduced defects in existing codebases [2] [3] [6]. DORA's finding that AI adoption is associated with higher code review speed but lower delivery stability suggests that faster generation can create a bottleneck downstream if review capacity and test coverage do not scale accordingly [8]. This helps explain why developers can feel more productive and satisfied while delivery metrics remain flat, as the cognitive relief of offloading boilerplate is real but does not necessarily reduce the total work required to ship safe, maintainable code [2] [5].

## Reliability and correctness: what benchmarks measure and miss

SWE-bench has become the primary measure of whether a coding agent can do real engineering work, by tasking models to resolve real GitHub issues across 12 popular Python repositories and verifying patches against existing test suites [19] [17]. The benchmark requires understanding and coordinating changes across multiple functions, classes, and files, interacting with execution environments, and processing long contexts [43]. Because the original dataset contains noisy and underspecified instances, OpenAI and Princeton curated SWE-bench Verified, a human-validated subset of 500 instances where annotators reviewed problem descriptions, test patches, and solvability [18] [20]. Even this curated subset is evaluated by applying the test patch and generated patch to the buggy repository and checking whether modified test files pass, a validation process that is intentionally narrow [38].

The limitation of that validation is central to reliability claims, as testing is rarely exhaustive and a patch may pass the relevant tests while failing to match developer expectations [38] [39]. An empirical study of plausible patches on SWE-bench Verified introduced a differential patch testing technique, PatchDiff, to generate tests that reveal behavioral differences between generated and human-written ground-truth patches, finding that evaluations suffer from plausible but incorrect patches that inflate resolution rates [38] [39]. A separate investigation of test overfitting on SWE-bench found that LLM-generated code patches can overfit to auto-generated reproduction tests, and that refining code to pass observed tests can break other functionality, with mitigation attempts reducing both overfitting and overall effectiveness [47]. The evidence suggests that passing the benchmark's tests is a necessary but not sufficient condition for correctness, and that reported pass rates should be interpreted as an upper bound on true issue resolution [38] [47].

Complementary work on test generation, SWT-Bench, proposes a benchmark based on popular GitHub repositories with real-world issues, ground-truth patches, and golden tests, and finds that code agents designed for repair can outperform systems designed specifically for test generation at formalizing issues into test cases [11] [34]. Generated tests are shown to be an effective filter for proposed fixes, doubling the precision of SWE-Agent in that study, which underscores that test quality is both a capability and a bottleneck for autonomous repair [11]. The finding that repair-oriented agents can generate relevant tests surprisingly well indicates that the same models that produce fixes can also help validate them, but also that the quality of that validation depends heavily on the agent's ability to understand the issue beyond the existing test suite [11] [34].

Reported capability levels have risen rapidly on these benchmarks, which itself creates interpretation risk [21] [17]. One synthesis reports SWE-bench Verified climbing from 13% in early 2024 to 78% in May 2026, with top agents reaching 74-78% and TerminalBench top scores at 52-58%, while estimating real-world pull-request pass rates at 35-50% for top agents due to implicit conventions and reviewer expectations that benchmarks miss [21]. Another analysis notes that as of June 2026, Claude Fable 5 tops SWE-bench Verified at a reported 95.0%, but that 99 of 100 leaderboard entries are self-reported and that standardized harnesses show materially lower scores [45]. The gap between benchmark saturation and production acceptance suggests that headline pass rates overstate readiness for autonomous deployment without human review [21] [45]. The evidence also indicates that open-weight backed agents trail frontier closed-API agents by 25-40% on SWE-bench Verified, and that median time to PR for autonomous agents on medium-complexity tasks is 8-25 minutes, while ambient pair-programming agents show median time to acceptance of 30-90 seconds per suggestion [21].

The reliability picture is further complicated by the fact that SWE-bench Verified's human validation, while rigorous, still leaves an estimated error rate of 5-10% due to ambiguous samples, and that infrastructure incompatibilities lead independent evaluators to exclude some samples from re-runs [46] [44]. This means that even the most careful benchmark cannot fully capture the implicit requirements, style conventions, and system-wide invariants that human reviewers enforce in production, which helps explain why real-world acceptance rates lag benchmark scores by 20 to 40 points [21] [45]. For teams considering autonomous agents, the implication is that benchmark performance should be treated as a tier filter for capability, not as a predictor of merge rate without additional investment in review and testing [45] [21].

## Security: insecure code at scale and the trust deficit

Security evidence converges from user studies and large-scale model evaluations that AI-generated code frequently introduces vulnerabilities [12] [22]. A Stanford-led user study with 47 participants solving five security-related tasks across Python, JavaScript, and C found that participants with access to an AI assistant based on codex-davinci-002 wrote significantly less secure code than those without access, and were more likely to believe they had written secure code [12] [13]. The study found insecure solutions more often for four of five tasks when AI was available, and that participants who trusted the AI less and engaged more with prompt phrasing and parameter adjustment produced fewer vulnerabilities [12].

Large-scale testing reinforces the pattern across models and languages, with Veracode's 2025 GenAI Code Security Report evaluating 80 curated coding tasks across more than 100 large language models and finding that AI-generated code introduced security vulnerabilities in 45% of cases [22] [24]. The same report found that when given a choice between secure and insecure methods, models chose the insecure option 45% of the time, and that 45% of test cases introduced OWASP Top 10 vulnerabilities [22] [25]. Language-specific failure rates were highest for Java at 72% and significant for Python at 38%, JavaScript at 43%, and C# at 45% [24] [26]. Specific weakness categories showed even higher failure rates, with cross-site scripting at 86% and log injection at 88% [22].

An October 2025 update using the same 80-task benchmark found that most providers showed no meaningful improvement between July and October 2025, with Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.5 at 50% and Claude Opus 4.1 at 49%, while OpenAI's GPT-5 reasoning models achieved 72% for GPT-5 Mini and 70% for GPT-5, the first substantial jump recorded, attributed to internal reasoning similar to code review [36]. The report notes that larger, newer models did not generally improve security, and that the state of AI-generated code security shows no signs of improvement over time without explicit security requirements [23] [26]. The finding that reasoning models can improve security pass rates by 10 to 20 points suggests that additional inference-time deliberation can catch insecure patterns, but also that this improvement is currently isolated to one provider and does not reflect a broad industry trend [36] [26].

The security findings help explain production adoption patterns, where developers report using tools they do not trust to produce secure output [14] [8]. The DORA 2024 report found that 39% of respondents reported little to no trust in AI-generated code despite productivity gains, indicating a need to manage integration more thoughtfully [8]. Veracode's analysis frames the risk as amplified by vibe coding, where developers rely on AI without explicitly defining security requirements, and notes that AI also enables attackers to identify and exploit vulnerabilities more efficiently [23] [25]. The convergence between the Stanford user study and Veracode's large-scale testing on the 45% vulnerability rate, and between DORA and Stack Overflow on low trust, suggests that the security cost is not an edge case but a central characteristic of current AI-generated code that must be addressed through explicit prompting, automated scanning, and review processes [12] [22] [14].

The implications for production are material, as the same tools that accelerate generation also increase the volume of code that must be reviewed for security, and the vulnerabilities introduced are often in high-severity categories that are difficult to detect without specialized scanning [22] [35]. Veracode's guidance emphasizes embedding security in agentic workflows, using software composition analysis, and providing real-time feedback in IDEs, reflecting that the security gap cannot be closed by model improvements alone but requires changes to how AI-generated code is produced and validated [23] [35]. The fact that participants who engaged more deeply with prompt phrasing and provided more context produced more secure code suggests that developer skill in directing AI is a meaningful mitigator, but also that this skill is not uniformly distributed and adds to the verification burden [12].

## Production adoption: high usage, low trust, and workflow reality

Adoption metrics show near-universal exposure, with the Stack Overflow 2025 Developer Survey of 49,009 responses from 166 countries reporting 84% of developers now use or plan to use AI coding tools, up from 76% in 2024 and 70% in 2023 [14] [16]. Heavy reliance is also rising, with DORA 2025 reporting 90% adoption and a median of two hours daily working with AI, and 65% heavily relying on AI for software development [9]. Yet trust has moved in the opposite direction, with trust in AI accuracy dropping from 43% in 2024 to 33% in 2025, with 46% actively distrusting AI tools and only 3% highly trusting AI outputs [14] [15]. AI favorability fell from 77% in 2023 to 72% in 2024 and then to 60% in 2025, with the erosion rate doubling in one year [14].

The survey data also clarifies where AI is actually used in workflows, with developers reporting that AI handles research and documentation better than complex tasks, and that only around 4% believe AI truly excels at complexity while 29% say it struggles with complex tasks [15]. Early-career developers with 1-5 years of experience are the most enthusiastic daily users, while seasoned developers report the lowest high trust and highest high distrust, suggesting that expertise increases skepticism about output quality [15] [14]. This aligns with the finding that AI coding agents are described as evolving from code completion to chat assistants to autonomous agents that plan, execute, debug, and test, with claimed potential to improve productivity by 2x to 3x, a claim that field data does not consistently support [10] [5].

Production deployment context further tempers benchmark optimism, with one 2026 synthesis estimating median time to PR for autonomous agents at 8-25 minutes for medium-complexity tasks and 30-90 seconds per suggestion for ambient pair-programming agents, while noting that open-weight backed agents trail frontier closed-API agents by 25-40% on SWE-bench Verified [21]. The same analysis emphasizes that real-world pull-request pass rates are materially below benchmark scores because benchmarks miss implicit conventions and reviewer expectations [21]. The evidence suggests that the most common production use remains partial assistance—research, documentation, and code explanation—rather than fully autonomous feature development, and that heavy users are more likely to use AI for understanding codebases and debugging than for writing entire features [15] [14].

The trust crisis has direct implications for workflow design, as developers report using tools they fundamentally do not believe in, creating a 51-point gap between usage at 84% and trust at 33% [14]. This gap is described as market dysfunction, where pressure to use AI coexists with skepticism about its output, and where the people who know code best are most skeptical, signaling a quality issue rather than a perception issue [14] [15]. The DORA finding that 39% of respondents have little to no trust in AI-generated code, even as more than one-third report moderate to extreme productivity increases, reinforces that productivity and trust are not moving together and that managing AI integration requires explicit guidelines and open communication about its impact [8].

## Economics and ROI: the J-curve, verification tax, and maintenance burden

Economic assessment is framed by DORA's ROI of AI-assisted Software Development report, which provides a practical framework for translating engineering metrics into business value and is positioned as a follow-up to the 2025 State of AI-assisted Software Development report [37] [31]. The report's central argument is that AI acts as an amplifier, with the greatest returns coming not from tools themselves but from the quality of internal platforms, clarity of workflows, and alignment of teams, and that without this foundation AI creates localized pockets of productivity lost in downstream chaos [37] [32]. This directly echoes the 2025 finding that strong teams become better with AI while struggling teams see existing problems intensified [32].

A key concept in the ROI framework is the J-curve of value realization, where most organizations experience a temporary productivity dip before long-term gains, driven by three causes: the learning curve as teams adapt workflows, the verification tax imposed by reviewing AI-generated code, and the need to adapt downstream practices [37]. The verification tax is material because AI-generated code requires human review to catch the higher bug rates and security flaws documented in field and lab studies, and because review time itself was only modestly improved in DORA 2024 at 3.1% for a 25% adoption increase [8] [6] [22]. Guidance for ROI measurement recommends making DORA metrics the north star and tracking AI-specific signals as leading indicators, rather than relying on lines of code, raw PR counts, or commits as business KPIs [33].

Maintenance burden adds a longer-term cost dimension that is not captured in short-term speed metrics, with analysis of AI-generated code noting a 300% increase in circular dependencies and a 40% higher rate of certain maintenance issues, alongside architectural shortcuts that create distributed monoliths and hidden coupling [48]. The same analysis describes velocity creating compounding micro-entropy in error handling, logging, and dependency management, making codebases brittle and opaque, and notes that AI agents cannot infer undocumented business logic, baking incorrect assumptions into new services [48]. While these specific percentages come from a vendor-leaning analysis and should be treated as attributed claims rather than independent measurements, they align directionally with the DORA finding that AI magnifies existing technical and process weaknesses [48] [32]. The evidence suggests that each AI-assisted commit can introduce minor inconsistencies that compound over time, creating a maintenance burden that is more insidious than immediate defects because it degrades architectural integrity [48].

Pricing and cost predictability further complicate ROI, with Devin cited as an example where entry pricing is advertised at $20 per month but usage-based Agent Compute Unit billing can lead to solo-developer bills of $150 to $250 or more for moderate use, with credits spent on failed tasks not refunded [30] [27]. This creates a gap between advertised entry price and realized cost that must be modeled against the verification and rework costs identified in the J-curve [37] [30]. The DORA ROI framework emphasizes that teams should baseline DORA metrics before piloting, shrink pull requests, and add automation, and that if a pilot does not move lead time or deployment frequency, the bottleneck likely lies in reviews, QA, or release cadence rather than code generation speed [33] [37]. The evidence suggests that organizations that treat AI as a platform change—updating reviews, automating checks, retraining onboarding, and measuring with DORA and SPACE—are more likely to capture value than those that treat it as a faster keyboard [33] [32].

The economic picture is also shaped by the finding that AI adoption is associated with improvements in documentation quality and code review speed, but that these gains do not automatically translate into financial return without strong version control practices, small batches, and healthy data ecosystems [8] [40]. DORA's AI Capabilities Model identifies seven capabilities that magnify AI's positive impact, including user-centric focus, strong version control, AI-accessible internal data, and quality internal platforms, indicating that ROI depends on a system of practices rather than a single tool [40] [32]. The evidence suggests that the initial productivity dip is not a sign of failure but an expected phase that must be planned for, and that defending budget for AI requires conversation starters that link engineering metrics to business value through this broader system lens [31] [37].

## Vendor claims versus independent evidence: the benchmark credibility gap

Vendor claims and independent measurements diverge systematically across productivity and capability benchmarks [42] [45]. On productivity, GitHub's reported 55% faster completion and high satisfaction are vendor-led findings from a controlled experiment, while independent or field-oriented studies from METR and Uplevel find slower completion, no throughput gain, and higher bug rates, with DORA's independent survey research finding negative delivery impacts in 2024 before a more positive but conditional relationship in 2025 [2] [3] [6] [8].

On capability benchmarks, the gap is quantified through independent re-runs under standardized conditions [42] [44]. Epoch AI, which independently re-runs SWE-bench Verified on 484 of 500 samples using fixed token limits, consistent scaffolding, and standardized Docker environments, found that DeepSeek's self-reported 57.6% with an Agentless scaffold measured 33% under standardized conditions, a 24-point gap [42]. Epoch also reports that after methodology updates, most models now score close to vendor claims, but that GPT-5.1 scored 68% in their scaffold versus 76.3% reported by OpenAI, while GPT-5.2 scored 74%, indicating persistent variance even after improvements [44]. A separate analysis of SWE-bench Verified as of June 2026 notes that 99 of 100 leaderboard entries are self-reported, that Claude Fable 5 is reported at 95.0% on the vendor leaderboard, and that the same model family scores 51.9% on Scale's standardized SEAL board versus 69.2% vendor-reported on SWE-bench Pro, a 17.3-point gap within a single family [45].

Historical vendor claims illustrate the methodology mismatch problem, with Cognition reporting Devin at 13.86% on SWE-bench by evaluating on a randomly chosen 25% subsample of 570 of 2,294 issues, compared to baselines computed on the full dataset, and choosing the stronger assisted baseline numbers for comparison [28] [27]. Independent real-world testing cited in a 2026 review found only 3 of 20 tasks succeeding in an Answer.AI study, with 14 failures and unpredictability about which tasks would work, contrasting with Cognition's own metrics showing improving PR-merge rates [30]. The review also notes that all benchmark figures for newer Cognition models are company-reported and that independent verification on contamination-resistant evaluations is the standard before treating them as settled [29].

The accountability gap is structural, as benchmark submission is voluntary, self-reported, and unaudited, with no third party required to sign off before a number reaches the leaderboard, though Princeton now flags self-reported scores and Scale AI created SEAL as an independently-run alternative [41] [42]. OpenAI's decision to stop reporting SWE-bench Verified after discovering training-data contamination across frontier models is cited as a signal that the self-reporting era is ending, with enterprise buyers increasingly expected to demand verified scores as a procurement requirement [41] [45]. The evidence suggests that the same model can show a 10 to 20 point spread depending on the harness, and that the number that sells a model and the number that predicts results in a buyer's environment are rarely the same [45] [42].

Taken together, the evidence suggests that vendor-reported benchmark scores should be treated as attributed claims reflecting optimal scaffolding and prompting, while independent standardized evaluations provide the more conservative estimate of expected performance in a buyer's environment [41] [45] [42]. The finding that Epoch's updated tools and prompt improvements closed much of the gap for most models indicates that scaffolding quality is a major driver of variance, but also that this variance is not random noise but a systematic effect that can be exploited to inflate headline numbers [44] [45]. For procurement, the implication is to treat SWE-bench Verified as a pass/fail tier filter and to lean on standardized and private-codebase evaluations for purchase decisions, as these are measured the same way for every model and closer to proprietary work [45].

## Synthesis: what the evidence supports and what would change the assessment

The strongest supportable conclusion is that AI coding agents deliver measurable value in specific, well-scoped tasks such as boilerplate generation, documentation, and code explanation, where lab studies and self-reported satisfaction converge, but that this value does not reliably translate into faster delivery, higher quality, or more secure code at the team or organization level without substantial process investment [2] [8]. Independent field studies and the DORA program converge on the finding that throughput and stability can stagnate or decline when AI adoption outpaces testing, review, and platform maturity, while security studies converge on a 45% vulnerability introduction rate and a trust deficit that widens as adoption grows [6] [8] [22] [14]. Reliability claims based on SWE-bench are inflated by test incompleteness and self-reported scaffolding effects, with independent audits documenting 15-35 point gaps and meaningful false-positive rates that must be discounted when projecting production performance [42] [45] [38].

The largest uncertainty is the net economic effect after accounting for the verification tax, rework from higher bug and vulnerability rates, and long-term maintenance burden from architectural shortcuts, all of which are poorly quantified in vendor ROI narratives but central to DORA's J-curve framework [37] [48] [6]. Current evidence does not provide a quantitative framework that reliably converts benchmark pass rates or lab speed gains into delivery throughput, change failure rate, or mean time to restore at scale, and the available ROI guidance explicitly warns that gains are conditional on platform quality, small batches, and healthy data ecosystems [40] [33] [32]. The evidence also leaves open how quickly the security gap can be closed, as the only substantial improvement observed comes from one provider's reasoning models, while most other providers show no progress over the same period [36] [26].

The assessment would change with three types of evidence that are currently missing or limited: longitudinal, team-level trials that measure DORA metrics, defect escape rates, and security incidents over multiple release cycles rather than single tasks; independent, contamination-resistant, standardized evaluations of agents on private codebases with reviewer acceptance as the outcome, rather than test-suite pass rates alone; and published, audited cost data linking AI usage, review time, rework, and incident remediation to financial return, including the J-curve dip duration and magnitude [37] [21] [41]. Until such evidence is available, the prudent interpretation is that AI coding agents are best treated as accelerators for well-instrumented teams with strong review and testing discipline, not as autonomous substitutes for engineering judgment, and that procurement and adoption decisions should weight independent standardized scores and field defect data more heavily than vendor-reported speed and benchmark headlines [33] [32] [45]. The evidence suggests that the gap between announcements and production reality is not a temporary lag but a structural feature of how AI coding agents are currently built, measured, and deployed, and that closing it requires investment in the surrounding system of work rather than in the model alone [32] [37] [8].
