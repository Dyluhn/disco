# Causes of the Late Bronze Age Collapse: major theories and evidence

## Executive Summary

The Late Bronze Age collapse around 1200 BCE unraveled a tightly interlinked world stretching from the Aegean to Mesopotamia within a few decades, with palaces burned or abandoned, administrations silenced and long-distance exchange sharply curtailed [2] [1]. The interval most often cited is roughly 1200 to 1150 BCE, when Mycenaean palaces, the Hittite capital Hattusa, the port of Ugarit and numerous Levantine centers were destroyed or deserted, Linear B literacy disappeared in Greece, and Egypt repelled invasions but entered internal decline [2] [3] [13]. Modern scholarship treats this not as a single invasion but as a multifactorial systems failure in which climate stress, seismic damage, warfare and migration, trade disruption and institutional fragility converged and amplified one another [2] [14] [33].

No single theory accounts for the full geographic and chronological pattern, and the evidence base itself is uneven: destruction layers and abandonment horizons are widespread but not synchronous, textual archives are rich at Ugarit and Hattusa yet absent elsewhere, and paleoclimate proxies show a severe arid phase while also revealing regional variability and chronological imprecision [14] [35] [30]. The strongest convergence is around a cascade model: a brittle, highly centralized palatial economy dependent on stable harvests and on tin and copper moving over very long distances was exposed to simultaneous shocks, so that failure in one node propagated through diplomacy, provisioning and revenue [2] [32] [43]. Within that frame, the Sea Peoples are increasingly read as symptom as much as cause, earthquakes as a plausible contributor but methodologically difficult to isolate, and drought as well-supported in the southern Levant but not uniformly the decisive trigger elsewhere [14] [15] [26].

The current synthesis therefore emphasizes interaction rather than monocausality, differential resilience rather than uniform collapse, and continuing uncertainty about timing, causation and agency, with Cyprus, parts of the Levantine coast and Egypt illustrating continuity or transformation while inland palatial cores disappeared [21] [38] [36]. What would change the assessment is not another single-cause claim but tighter chronological control linking destruction horizons to climate and seismic events, and more direct evidence for how households and secondary centers experienced and responded to stress [39] [45].

## A world that worked until it did not

The Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean between about 1550 and 1200 BCE was characterized by unprecedented interconnection among palace-centered polities [2]. Diplomatic correspondence such as the Amarna Letters documents exchanges of gifts, marriages and treaties among the kings of Egypt, Babylon, Assyria, Hatti and Mycenaean palaces [2]. Long-distance movement of tin, copper, ivory, glass and luxury goods linked the Aegean, Anatolia, the Levant, Mesopotamia and Egypt, with tin in particular sourced from very distant regions because bronze requires roughly ten percent tin alloyed with copper and most palatial centers had no local supply [6] [32]. Palatial economies at sites like Mycenae, Knossos, Hattusa and Ugarit functioned as redistributive centers controlling production, storage and elite consumption, and literacy in Linear B and cuneiform was largely confined to palace scribes [2] [17].

That efficiency came with brittleness [2]. The system depended on stable climate, secure routes, elite alliances and the absence of major disruptions, and its very interdependence meant that distant disturbances could cascade [3] [43]. When harvests failed, palaces could not collect the grain needed to pay scribes, soldiers and craftsmen, and the administrative apparatus began to crumble [18]. The archaeological record for the decades before 1200 BCE shows signs of strain rather than sudden surprise: at Mycenae and Tiryns, walls were massively expanded and deep fortified cisterns were built to secure water inside citadels in case of siege [1]. At Pylos, the final Linear B tablets, baked in the destruction fire, record last-minute military orders tracking watchers guarding the coast and the urgent deployment of rowers to maritime lookouts, signaling an imminent threat from the sea [1] [13].

The chronology of breakdown is compressed but not instantaneous [3]. Major destructions cluster between about 1200 and 1190 BCE, including the burning of Mycenaean palaces at Pylos, Mycenae and Tiryns, the abandonment and sacking of Hattusa, and the fiery destruction of Ugarit, Alalakh, Emar and Troy VIIa [2]. Between about 1180 and 1150 BCE Egypt repelled two major invasions under Ramesses III but suffered internal decline, trade dried up and bronze became scarce, accelerating adoption of iron [2]. Taken together, the evidence describes a cascade of destruction and abandonment within roughly fifty years, followed by sharply reduced literacy, cessation of monumental building and the onset of what was once called a Dark Age in Greece from about 1100 to 800 BCE [2] [3].

## How the collapse is documented

Destruction and abandonment provide the most visible horizon [13]. In Mycenaean Greece, sites such as Pylos, Mycenae and Tiryns show violent destruction and abandonment, with Linear B tablets at Pylos indicating emergency preparations against attack [13]. In Anatolia, Hattusa was burned and deserted around 1200 BCE, with a subsequent lack of written records suggesting complete breakdown of administrative structures [13]. In the Levant, Ugarit was completely destroyed and never reoccupied, while excavations at Megiddo, Hazor and Lachish show layers consistent with widespread warfare [13]. Yet systematic comparison of these layers weakens any single-invasion narrative, because they are spread across decades and several key sites show abandonment rather than battle damage [14].

Textual archives add administrative detail but also bias [14]. At Ugarit, the house of the merchant Urtenu preserved some 650 tablets documenting copper, wood and other exports and imports from Cyprus and Egypt, along with diplomatic correspondence with Egypt, Assyria, Beirut and Hatti [22]. Between about 1200 and 1185 BCE that correspondence turns ominous, with polite requests becoming desperate pleas as hunger, *biru* in Akkadian, spreads, and with grain requisitions, troop movements and unanswered appeals for naval help recorded in the final months [22] [14]. Hittite letters similarly describe food shortage as a present factor in the empire's final years, though what instigated it and what role it played remains debated [23]. The term *mānaḫātu* in letters from Hittite officials at Ugarit appears to denote both a vassal tribute in grain staples owed to the empire and a related administrative category, underscoring how provisioning obligations tied peripheral cities to the Hittite core [24].

Paleoclimate and archaeometric data provide an independent but lower-resolution check [26]. High-resolution pollen analysis from cores drilled beneath the Sea of Galilee, sampled at roughly 40-year intervals and dated by radiocarbon on short-lived terrestrial material, reconstructs a dramatic arid phase [25] [26]. The driest phase is placed at about 1250 to 1100 BCE, coinciding with the collapse horizon, with urban centers in the Levant facing destruction during crisis years from the mid-13th century to about 1100 BCE and with subsequent Iron I recovery in settlement and olive cultivation [26] [25]. Greenland ice-core temperatures show a sharp increase during the same period, and the authors argue that drought and famine attested in texts from the mid-13th through the second half of the 12th century help explain both the crisis and the quick highland recovery afterward [26].

## Climate, drought and famine as stressors

Climate-driven drought recurs in both textual and scientific evidence, but its weight and mechanism are contested [35] [11]. A 2026 modeling study using the EC-Earth3 climate model to simulate 8,000 years of climate history argues that extreme droughts coinciding with the collapse were not isolated accidents but the result of multiple long-term and short-term processes aligning, including gradual drying across the Eastern Mediterranean and naturally occurring Atlantic variability that together pushed the region beyond critical environmental thresholds [12] [11]. The researchers reconstructed rainfall, temperature, soil moisture, vegetation and ocean-atmosphere circulation, concluding that thousands of years of gradual change preceded exceptionally severe dry periods between roughly 1300 and 900 BCE [12].

Pollen evidence strengthens the case for severe aridity in the southern Levant while also showing that the signal is not uniform [26]. Slight differences between the Sea of Galilee and Dead Sea records derive from the Dead Sea's position at the meeting point of Mediterranean, semi-arid and desert environments, making it more vulnerable to climate change, and earlier fluctuations such as a reduction in olive values in the Early Bronze Age are linked to geopolitical shifts rather than climate alone [26]. The Late Bronze arid phase, by contrast, is described as the most striking feature, with a dramatic decline in arboreal pollen and olive trees, and is integrated with archaeological settlement data to argue for interconnected climate crises across the eastern Mediterranean [26] [27].

Cross-validation comes from isotope records, though with important nuance [30]. A high-resolution oxygen and carbon isotope record from Stalagmite S1 in Mavri Trypa Cave near Pylos, dated by 24 U-Th dates with average precision of about 26 years, provides one of the most robust paleoclimate records for the end of the Late Bronze Age in the Peloponnese [30]. The oxygen isotope record shows generally wetter conditions at the time the Palace of Nestor at Pylos was destroyed at the transition from Late Helladic IIIB to IIIC, about 3150 to 3130 years before present, but a brief drier period around 3200 years before present may have disrupted a Mycenaean agricultural system already operating close to its limit, with gradually developing aridity after 3150 years before present reducing crop yields and eroding the basis for re-establishing central authority [30]. This pattern both supports and complicates a simple drought-causes-collapse narrative: it confirms climatic variability around the destruction horizon while showing that the immediate destruction at Pylos occurred under wetter conditions, with aridity acting more as a post-destruction constraint on recovery [30].

Textual evidence for famine is most explicit for the Hittite world and Ugarit [23] [22]. Hittite king Šuppiluliuma II, reigning about 1207 to 1178 BCE, left inscriptions describing attempts to secure grain shipments from Lukka that never arrived, and the inland capital Hattusa, with limited water resources, depopulated rapidly before being abandoned and burned by about 1180 BCE [18]. At Ugarit, letters speak of hunger spreading across the landscape and request that even remainders be sent, while skeletal remains from the period show increased malnutrition, growth stunting and infectious disease, and Egyptian tomb paintings around 1180 BCE depict emaciated refugees begging for grain [18] [22]. The convergence of pollen, isotope and textual data therefore strengthens the inference of severe food stress, but the divergence in timing and local hydrology cautions against treating drought as a uniform, synchronous cause [30] [26].

## Earthquakes and the earthquake-storm debate

Seismic explanations have moved from single-event catastrophism to a debated storm hypothesis [4] [15]. Nur and Cline in 2000 proposed that a cluster of large earthquakes along the Eastern Mediterranean plate boundaries — the Dead Sea Transform, East Anatolian and Hellenic Arc faults — between about 1225 and 1175 BCE contributed to destructions from Troy to Ugarit [4] [15]. The concept draws on modern analogues such as the North Anatolian Fault sequence from 1939 to 1999, where magnitude 6.7 to 7.9 earthquakes migrated westward over 60 years, illustrating how stress transfer can produce a storm of events over decades [4].

Archaeological indicators cited include directional wall collapse, offset blocks, leaning and displaced walls and large blocks thrown from citadel circuits, reported at Troy VIh, Mycenae, Tiryns, Midea, Knossos and Ugarit [4]. At Troy VIh, dated to about 1300 BCE, excavations identified widespread seismic destruction followed by rapid rebuilding as Troy VIIa, the probable Homeric Troy, with the suggestion that earthquake damage may have weakened defenses and made the site vulnerable to attack, though that interpretation remains debated [4]. For the Argive Basin, field data from Tiryns and Midea, both founded on bedrock, have been used to hypothesize synchronized co-seismic damage at about 1190 BCE at the end of the palatial period [5].

The limits of this evidence are foregrounded in the same literature [45] [46]. Archaeoseismology relies on a demanding methodology: reconstructing local stratigraphy to position a destruction layer, analyzing deformations and collapsed material, investigating local geology and ground-motion amplification, and estimating dynamic excitation [45]. Relating effects unambiguously to earthquakes remains challenging in both space and time, and quantitative archaeoseismology is still developing methods to turn sites into seismoscopes that can assess site-specific ground motion [46]. From a quantitative perspective, the hypothesis of synchronized damage at Tiryns and Midea has been criticized for overlooking local site effects and other factors, illustrating that earthquake damage is difficult to distinguish definitively from military destruction in the archaeological record [5] [15]. Syntheses therefore treat earthquake sequences as able to explain some destruction patterns but not by themselves the end of archives, long-distance diplomacy and political systems across the whole region [19].

## War, migration and the Sea Peoples

Warfare is archaeologically visible but not universally present [13] [14]. Destruction layers at Pylos, Mycenae, Tiryns, Hattusa, Ugarit, Megiddo, Hazor and Lachish are often associated with fire and abandonment, and Linear B tablets at Pylos point to emergency military mobilization [13] [1]. Yet the synthesis that destruction layers are spread across decades and that several key sites show abandonment rather than battle damage weakens any model of a single coordinated invasion [14]. Claims that a single volcanic eruption caused the collapse lack chronological fit, with proposed eruption dates not aligning with the destruction sequence and ice-core volcanic signatures not supporting a catastrophic event in the 1200 to 1150 BCE window [32].

The Sea Peoples occupy the most contested space between text and archaeology [8] [29]. Egyptian hieroglyphic inscriptions from the reign of Ramesses III at Medinet Habu vividly describe a confederation of seafaring invaders, naming groups including the Peleset, Shekelesh, Shardana, Lukka, Denyen and Tjeker, and depicting them migrating with families in ox-carts while warships battled Egyptian forces in the Delta [9] [8] [10]. The term Sea Peoples itself is a 19th-century coinage by Emmanuel de Rougé to describe these Egyptian records, primarily at Medinet Habu, which identify at least nine groups [10]. The Peleset are commonly equated with the biblical Philistines, and their distinctive Mycenaean-style IIIC:1b pottery appears suddenly around 1175 BCE at Ashkelon and Gath, with later ancient DNA studies indicating European ancestry in Philistine burials at Ashkelon [18] [38].

Two opposing readings of this evidence coexist [14] [32]. One treats the Sea Peoples as a coalition that devastated the Mediterranean, emerging around 1250 BCE after droughts in the western Mediterranean destroyed crops, caused famine, halted trade and weakened kingdoms, prompting mass migration to fertile river regions that could not sustain the influx [8]. The other, now dominant in specialist literature, reinterprets them as displaced populations — refugees, raiders and mercenaries from multiple origins in the Aegean, Anatolia and the western Mediterranean — set in motion by the same stressors that destabilized the major powers, shifting them from cause to symptom [32] [38] [14]. In this view, the Sea Peoples appear as a central element in a cascade that gathers force as shocks pile up, rather than as a unified invasion force [31].

Critical reassessments emphasize circularity and bias [29] [28]. Recent work challenges assumptions about migration, material culture and timeline by reexamining the use of terms like cut off, the fall of the Hittite Empire, the date of Ramesses III's confrontation in light of the Amara West Stele, and the association of Philistine settlement with Late Helladic IIIC:1b pottery [29]. Broader critiques show how Egyptian textual designations and archaeological assumptions have been shaped by modern geopolitical and cultural biases, and how the Sea Peoples are attested only in Egyptian sources that may exaggerate external threats to justify military spending [28] [32]. The simplistic narrative of barbarian Sea Peoples destroying civilization is therefore treated as contradicted by evidence that many cities show no military destruction and that the collapse was multi-causal [32].

## Trade, metals and systemic fragility

Interdependence is central to current models because bronze itself required interdependence [6] [7]. Copper is widely available, but tin is rare and unevenly distributed, with major sources debated between Afghanistan and western sources such as Cornwall, the Erzgebirge or Iberia, and with Bronze Age tin ingots from Mediterranean wrecks and the Israeli coast matching southwestern British tin in recent isotopic work [6]. The Uluburun wreck, dating to about 1320 BCE, illustrates the typical cargo of a Late Bronze Age merchant vessel: about ten tons of Cypriot copper oxhide ingots, about one ton of tin ingots, Canaanite jars with terebinth resin, ivory, Egyptian glass ingots and Mycenaean pottery [18].

When palatial customers vanished, the network effects were severe [7] [43]. Around 1200 BCE eight major civilizations unraveled within a few decades, cities burned, palace economies dissolved and regional trade routes stopped operating, ushering in a centuries-long dark age [7]. Eric Cline's systems-collapse model, widely influential though difficult to test rigorously, argues that the Late Bronze Age world constituted an interdependent network where disruption in one node — grain supply, tin trade, diplomatic exchange — propagated cascading failures analogous to modern supply-chain collapse [32] [33]. Complexity theory and Joseph Tainter's work on diminishing returns to complexity are invoked to explain why highly complex societies become vulnerable to stressors, and the Bronze Age collapse is presented as the earliest well-documented example of systemic cascading failure with relevance to modern globalized systems [33] [44].

The mechanism is not simply that tin ran out, but that loss of key cities destabilized the trade network and undermined state revenue, leading to further rebellion, migration and warfare, with enough cities destroyed to undermine the economic, cultural and political fabric that held the Bronze Age together [43]. Tin trade disruption is therefore treated as a potentially critical trigger: if supply was interrupted by collapse of intermediary states or route disruption, armies and economies dependent on bronze would have been immediately compromised [32]. Yet the same interdependence that created fragility also created resilience in some nodes, as seen in Cyprus, complicating any deterministic reading [21] [20].

## Internal dynamics and social strain

Internal rebellion and elite fragmentation are plausible pathways but unevenly evidenced [19] [16]. Linear B tablets from Pylos document extreme centralization, with textile workers, bronzesmiths and agricultural producers recorded as dependent laborers administered through the palace [16]. Jan Driessen has proposed that the collapse partly reflected internal revolt against oppressive palace administrations, while the Hittite Empire showed signs of internal fragmentation with vassal revolts and succession disputes in its final decades, and Egyptian records under Ramesses III document a workers' strike at Deir el-Medina in 1158 BCE, the first recorded labor strike in history [16]. Stratified palace societies could be destabilized by famine, debt, corvée labor, mercenary unrest or succession struggles, but direct evidence for peasant revolt is limited, so this is best treated as a plausible social pathway rather than a proven universal cause [19].

Military and technological change is similarly qualified [19]. Iron became more important after the collapse because iron ore is more widely available than tin, and Robert Drews influentially argued that changes in infantry tactics undermined chariot elites [19] [38]. However, iron did not instantly replace bronze everywhere; the shift was gradual and should not be treated as a simple cause, and volcanic or short-term atmospheric shocks, while possibly aggravating vulnerability, have uncertain dating and regional impact [19]. The overall picture is therefore one in which internal stresses — administrative rigidity, inequality, labor demands and elite competition — interacted with external shocks, but where the evidentiary basis for revolt as a primary driver remains thinner than for climate or trade disruption [16] [14].

## Why the collapse was not uniform

Regional variation is decisive for testing any theory [38] [21]. Egypt survived the crisis though weakened, repelling invasions under Ramesses III while experiencing internal decline, whereas Hattusa was abandoned and the Hittite language disappeared, and Mycenaean palaces were destroyed with population declines estimated as high as 75 percent in some areas [2] [17] [38]. Cyprus presents the sharpest paradox: despite depending almost entirely on long-distance maritime trade as the Mediterranean's primary supplier of standardized thirty-kilogram copper oxhide ingots, its coastal centers like Enkomi, Kition and Palaepaphos expanded rapidly during Late Cypriot IIIA, about 1200 to 1100 BCE, entering an era of economic growth and industrial transformation while mainland centers burned [21] [20].

Cypriot continuity was not isolation but adaptation [21] [20]. When royal customers vanished, the island should have faced bankruptcy, yet it redirected copper exports, with fragments of Cypriot oxhide ingots found across Nuragic sanctuaries and hoards in Sardinia and with Cypriot metallurgical iconography adopted in Sardinian bronze statuettes, the *bronzetti*, beginning in the 12th century BCE [21] [20]. Some Levantine coastal cities such as Byblos and Sidon also continued operating, and the Neo-Assyrians persisted by shifting from trade to conquest, while surviving Hittite elements in northern Syria relied on different strategies, illustrating that no two states were alike in resilience [38] [43]. Even within the Aegean, surveys covering Attica, central Greece, the islands, Crete and the Peloponnese show varied impacts, with some contributors stressing continuity in socio-economic changes predating the collapse rather than abrupt shifts, and with 26 proposed causes listed for the Mycenaean collapse alone [41].

This variability undermines monocausal determinism and supports a systemic-risk framing [43] [45]. Interdependence may be a blessing during stability but an Achilles heel if shocks are large enough, and thresholds for critical transition hinge on both long-term trends in interconnectedness and inequality and on the specific shocks a system faces [43]. The fact that Cyprus transformed rather than collapsed, and that Egypt endured while Hatti did not, suggests that geography, economic structure and adaptive governance mediated the same regional stressors differently [21] [38].

## What the field now agrees on, and where it diverges

Historiography has moved decisively away from single-cause invasion narratives toward multi-causal, systems-oriented explanations, but without claiming a final solution [36] [39]. Knapp and Manning in 2016 concluded that explanations are legion — migrations, predations, political struggles, system collapse, center-periphery inequalities, climatic change, natural disasters, disease — and that there has never been an overarching explanation accounting for all changes, some occurring at different times from the mid-13th through the 12th centuries BCE [34] [35]. They emphasized the ambiguity of material, textual, climatic and chronological evidence and the difficulty of disentangling background noise from boundary conditions and cause from effect, calling for continuing collaborative work [39] [40].

That consensus is echoed across syntheses [33] [14]. Cline's 2014 model argues that no single factor — drought, earthquakes, Sea Peoples, internal revolt or trade disruption — was sufficient alone, but their simultaneous convergence overwhelmed interconnected systems, a perfect-storm model that draws on resilience theory and systemic risk [33] [43]. Middleton's work critiques sensationalist collapse myths and stresses that traditional notions of collapse are overly simplistic, that many communities displayed resilience and made choices, and that collapse involved slow changes as well as rapid failures, with human agency and intrasocietal conflict as recurring themes [37] [47] [42]. The volume *Collapse and Transformation* explicitly presents 24 papers without forcing a single interpretation, treating the Mycenaean collapses as plural and regionally variable [41].

Divergences remain sharp on three points [15] [29] [30]. First, the earthquake storm remains credible but debated and supported, with archaeological evidence of seismic damage at multiple sites yet persistent difficulty separating seismic from anthropogenic destruction and questions about synchronicity [4] [5]. Second, the Sea Peoples debate pits textual visibility against archaeological invisibility, with Egyptian sources providing the only direct attestation and with recent scholarship advocating deconstruction of ethnonationalist frameworks and Balkanist interpretations [28] [29]. Third, climate's role is identified as relevant but ambiguous and intertwined with human actions, with high-resolution pollen and isotope records strengthening the case for severe drought in the southern Levant while also showing wetter conditions at Pylos at the moment of destruction and gradual aridity afterward [34] [30].

Methodological cautions reinforce this measured stance [45] [46]. Archaeoseismology's usefulness is limited by lack of continuous methodological discussion and by the need for interdisciplinary validation, and overprecise chronologies risk creating false coherence across regions where dating remains coarse [45] [39]. Taken together, the evidence suggests that the Late Bronze Age collapse is best understood as a human-induced crisis with multiple material, social and cultural realities, not a single natural disaster or invasion [40] [36].

## Synthesis: a cascade, not a single blow

The weight of evidence supports a cascade in which no single shock was sufficient but their interaction was [33] [14]. A hyper-connected Late Bronze Age world of palace economies, diplomatic marriages and tin-dependent bronze production created both prosperity and systemic risk, so that drought-induced harvest failure, seismic damage, warfare and migration, and interruption of copper and tin flows could reinforce one another and compress the window for recovery [2] [6] [43]. Destruction horizons at Pylos, Mycenae, Hattusa, Ugarit and Troy cluster within decades but are not synchronous, and the coexistence of violent burning, abandonment and continuity within the same region argues against a single invasion or single natural disaster [2] [14].

Within that cascade, the relative weight of factors varies by subregion and by evidentiary strength [30] [21]. Drought and famine are most strongly documented for the southern Levant and the Hittite heartland, where pollen, textual pleas for grain and rapid depopulation of an inland capital converge, yet the Peloponnesian isotope record shows that local hydrology and timing matter and that aridity may have hindered recovery more than triggered the initial destruction [26] [18] [30]. Earthquakes plausibly contributed to damage at several citadels but remain methodologically contested and insufficient to explain the collapse of administration and literacy [15] [45]. The Sea Peoples are best understood as both actors and victims: Egyptian reliefs and Philistine material culture document real movements of people, but the groups were diverse, their origins debated, and their appearance is increasingly read as a consequence of the same food and political stresses that undermined palaces [38] [32] [14].

The decisive uncertainty is chronological and causal resolution [39] [45]. Without tighter dating that can place a specific destruction layer, a specific arid interval and a specific seismic event in secure sequence at a given site, it remains difficult to distinguish trigger from amplifier and to test whether shocks were truly simultaneous or merely overlapping within the coarse resolution of radiocarbon and ceramic dating [35] [30]. What would change the conclusion is not another monocausal claim but integrated, site-level chronologies combining high-precision radiocarbon, dendrochronology where available, and well-provenanced paleoclimate proxies, alongside more evidence from non-palatial settlements and households that reveals how resilience or failure was experienced below the palace level [39] [41]. Until then, the most supportable judgment is that the Late Bronze Age collapse was a systemic, human-influenced failure of an interdependent world, in which environmental stress and institutional fragility made societies vulnerable to shocks that, taken alone, they might have survived [40] [36].
